> **Visa** **Chargeback** **Reason** **Codes**
>
> Visa groups its chargeback reason codes into broad categories (10 =
> Fraud, 11 = Authorization, 12 = Processing Errors, 13 = Consumer
> Disputes). Below are **all** **current** **consumer** **dispute**
> **reason** **codes** with their descriptions, required evidence for
> representment (per the latest Visa guidelines), and additional
> supporting evidence recommended to strengthen a response:
>
> • **10.1** **–** **EMV** **Liability** **Shift** **Counterfeit**
> **Fraud:** The cardholder claims an in-person transaction was
> fraudulent (card was counterfeit). **Required** **Evidence:** Proof
> that the transaction was conducted at a chip-enabled terminal and read
> from the chip (indicating EMV compliance) 1 . For example, provide
> documentation showing the card was chip-read and transaction data
> (TVR) confirming EMV processing. **Recommended** **Evidence:**
> Transaction logs or authorization records showing chip data (or
> fallback procedures), and a copy of the signed receipt or other
> Cardholder Verification Method results to demonstrate the cardholder’s
> participation 2 .
>
> • **10.2** **–** **EMV** **Liability** **Shift** **Non-Counterfeit**
> **Fraud:** The cardholder claims a fraudulent transaction with a chip
> card that wasn’t counterfeit (e.g. lost/stolen scenario). **Required**
> **Evidence:** Proof the transaction was conducted at an EMV
> *PIN*-compliant terminal, or otherwise that the issuer, not the
> merchant, is liable (e.g. chip + PIN used) 3 . Provide evidence the
> card was chip-and-PIN read (e.g. terminal logs showing PIN entry).
> **Recommended** **Evidence:** Signed receipts or surveillance (for
> chip-and-signature), and any verification that the proper PIN was
> entered or ID was checked, to reinforce that the cardholder (or an
> authorized user) participated in the transaction.
>
> • **10.3** **–** **Other** **Fraud** **–** **Card-Present**
> **Environment:** The cardholder claims a card-present transaction was
> not authorized by them (magstripe read or key-entered, not an EMV
> chip). **Required** **Evidence:** Proof the card was present and
> properly authorized. For instance, a copy of the authorization
> approval and evidence of a valid card imprint or swipe 2 . If the card
> was swiped, provide the authorization record showing the magnetic
> stripe data was read and a valid authorization code obtained 2 .
> **Recommended** **Evidence:** A copy of the signed sales receipt or
> electronic signature capture, any security photos or videos of the
> customer at the point of sale, and any additional proof of cardholder
> identity (if collected) to demonstrate the legitimate cardholder
> completed the purchase.
>
> • **10.4** **–** **Other** **Fraud** **–** **Card-Absent**
> **Environment:** The cardholder claims a card-not-present (CNP)
> transaction (e-commerce, mail/phone order) was unauthorized.
> **Required** **Evidence:** Evidence that the cardholder indeed
> authorized or participated in the transaction. This may include proof
> of 3-D Secure authentication (Visa Secure) or other verification. For
> example, show that the transaction was Visa Secure authenticated at
> the time of purchase 4 or that you received an identity verification
> match (AVS, CVV) on the transaction. **Recommended** **Evidence:**
> “Compelling evidence” per Visa rules – e.g. proof of the same card
> successfully used in prior transactions that were not disputed,
> delivery confirmation to the cardholder’s address, IP address/device
> match with previous known customer orders, email correspondence with
> the cardholder, etc. Providing such additional documentation can help
> prove the legitimate cardholder was involved 5 6 .
>

>
> • **10.5** **–** **Visa** **Fraud** **Monitoring** **Program:** A
> dispute initiated due to excessive fraud (Visa’s monitoring program
> triggered). **Required** **Evidence:** This is a fraud-related
> chargeback that occurs when thresholds are exceeded. To respond, the
> merchant must show evidence contradicting the fraud claim (similar to
> 10.4) or demonstrate that fraud controls were in place.
> **Recommended** **Evidence:** Any compelling evidence as with other
> fraud disputes (prior transaction history, customer communications) to
> prove the charge is legitimate. Often, *10.5* *disputes* *cannot* *be*
> *overturned* *without* *clear* *proof* *of* *cardholder*
> *participation*, given they result from fraud monitoring flags.
>
> • **11.1** **–** **Card** **Recovery** **Bulletin** **(CRB)** **/**
> **Hot** **Card:** The transaction was completed with a card number
> listed on a stolen card or account recovery bulletin. **Required**
> **Evidence:** Proof that the transaction was authorized *after*
> checking for card status, or that an authorization approval was
> obtained despite the card being listed. In practice, issuers rarely
> use this code now. Merchants should show evidence of a valid
> authorization code. **Recommended** **Evidence:** Transaction logs
> showing the authorization request and approval timestamp,
> demonstrating compliance with acceptance procedures.
>
> • **11.2** **–** **Declined** **Authorization:** The merchant
> processed a transaction after receiving a decline response or without
> attempting authorization. **Required** **Evidence:** Proof that a
> valid approval was obtained for the transaction amount. For example,
> documentation of an authorization approval code covering the full
> amount of the charge 7 8 . If an approval for the full amount wasn’t
> obtained, proof that an allowable exception applies (such as an ofline
> approval within limit). **Recommended** **Evidence:** If the charge
> was split or retried, provide records of all authorization
> attempts/responses. If the issuer’s decline was in error, include any
> communication from the issuer reversing the decline or indicating the
> transaction was actually authorized.
>
> • **11.3** **–** **No** **Authorization** **/** **Late**
> **Presentment:** The issuer claims the merchant did not obtain
> authorization or submitted the transaction too late. (Visa merged
> reason 12.1 into 11.3 for transactions after April 2024.) **Required**
> **Evidence:** Proof of a valid authorization for the transaction, or
> proof the transaction was submitted within required timeframes.
> Provide the authorization code or receipts showing the date of
> transaction and date of settlement to demonstrate timely processing 9
> 10 . If late presentment is alleged but the transaction was on time,
> supply the transaction log showing the processing date 10 .
> **Recommended** **Evidence:** Copies of system logs or processor
> confirmation showing when the batch was transmitted. If authorization
> was attempted but not received due to system issues, include any
> correspondence with your processor about network outages or downtimes
> as context.
>
> • **12.2** **–** **Incorrect** **Transaction** **Code:** The
> transaction was processed with an incorrect transaction type code
> (e.g., refund processed as sale, or a debit as credit). **Required**
> **Evidence:** Documentation proving the transaction was submitted with
> the correct code or that the coding error did not occur. For example,
> show that the transaction was indeed processed as a credit or debit
> appropriately 11 . **Recommended** **Evidence:** Terminal reports or
> screenshots from your point-of-sale showing how the transaction was
> entered. If a mistake occurred but was corrected, provide evidence of
> the correction (such as a reversing entry or credit).
>
> • **12.3** **–** **Incorrect** **Currency:** The charge was processed
> in the wrong currency or with incorrect currency conversion.
> **Required** **Evidence:** Proof that the correct currency was used as
> agreed by the cardholder. For instance, documentation that the
> cardholder chose dynamic currency conversion (DCC) or that the
> transaction currency matched the price quoted 12 . **Recommended**
> **Evidence:** Customer receipts showing currency selection, any DCC
> consent confirmation, and
>

>
> backend records of the transaction currency and amount. If an error
> was made but subsequently fixed (e.g. a refund or re-charge), provide
> those records.
>
> • **12.4** **–** **Incorrect** **Account** **Number:** The card number
> or account identifier submitted was invalid or not the one intended by
> the cardholder. **Required** **Evidence:** Proof that the card number
> on the disputed charge matches the card number presented by the
> customer. Typically, a copy of the transaction receipt showing the PAN
> (truncated) and that it aligns with the card used 13 . **Recommended**
> **Evidence:** Copies of any account verification or imprint of the
> card (for card-present) to show the card number was read correctly. If
> the wrong account was charged due to data entry error, evidence that
> the correct account was subsequently credited can be helpful.
>
> • **12.5** **–** **Incorrect** **Amount:** The cardholder was charged
> an amount different from what they agreed to (e.g., a \$50 purchase
> was processed as \$500). **Required** **Evidence:** Proof that the
> transaction amount is correct as authorized by the cardholder. A
> signed receipt or invoice reflecting the disputed amount is key 14 .
> For example, provide the original signed receipt or order confirmation
> for \$X to show the amount was approved by the customer 14 .
> **Recommended** **Evidence:** Itemized invoices or order forms, any
> emails or communications from the customer acknowledging the correct
> amount, or proof of any agreed-upon addendums (if the amount changed
> with customer consent). If a data entry error occurred but the
> customer was issued a credit for the difference, include proof of that
> credit.
>
> • **12.6** **–** **Duplicate** **Processing** **/** **Paid** **by**
> **Other** **Means:** The cardholder was charged more than once for the
> same purchase, or paid via another method but also charged on their
> Visa card. **Required** **Evidence:** Proof that the disputed charge
> is not a duplicate or that the cardholder *did* *not* actually pay by
> other means. For duplicate billing claims, show that two similar
> charges represent two separate transactions or orders (provide
> invoices or receipts for each) 15 . For “paid by other means,”
> demonstrate that no other payment was received for that transaction
> (e.g. no record of cash or check for the same purchase) 16 .
> **Recommended** **Evidence:** Any internal records or correspondence
> indicating the customer’s intent to make two distinct purchases. In a
> “paid by other means” scenario, include copies of your accounts
> showing the absence of an alternate payment, or if the customer did
> pay twice, proof you have already refunded one of the payments.
> Communication with the cardholder (emails, etc.) acknowledging the
> payment method can also support your case.
>
> • **12.7** **–** **Invalid** **Data:** Some transaction data was
> invalid (e.g., an expired card, invalid expiration date, CVV mismatch
> where it should have been matched, etc.). **Required** **Evidence:**
> Proof that all transaction data was valid and properly entered. For
> example, if an expiration date was allegedly wrong, show the
> authorization response approving the transaction with the data
> provided. If the dispute is for other invalid data (like an incorrect
> merchant category or an addendum issue), show documentation of correct
> data submission. **Recommended** **Evidence:** System printouts of the
> transaction details sent to Visa (including AVS/CVV response codes if
> relevant), any logs from your payment gateway, and evidence that the
> cardholder’s details on the order matched the account (e.g., AVS match
> results). Essentially, any extra data that confirms the transaction
> information was accurate can bolster your response.
>
> • **13.1** **–** **Merchandise/Services** **Not** **Received:** The
> cardholder claims they paid for goods or services that were never
> received or made available by the agreed date. **Required**
> **Evidence:** Proof that the merchandise or service was delivered or
> provided as agreed. The strongest evidence is a delivery confirmation
> or pickup record signed or acknowledged by the cardholder (or an
> authorized recipient) by the agreed-upon date 17 18 . Alternatively,
> if the delivery date has not yet passed, provide documentation of the
> expected delivery date to show it’s not due yet
>
> 3
>
> 19 . Other acceptable evidence includes proof that *services* were
> made available or rendered (e.g. login records for digital goods, or
> an appointment log for services). **Recommended** **Evidence:**
> Shipping documentation with tracking numbers and delivery confirmation
> (showing date, time, and address), customer correspondence (emails or
> texts) confirming receipt or scheduling of delivery, and evidence of
> any usage of the service or product (for example, activation logs for
> digital products). If the cardholder cancelled the service/order,
> provide proof of the cancellation date and any refund or cancellation
> policy communicated 20 . For special scenarios (partial shipments,
> backorders, etc.), include records of customer communication agreeing
> to adjusted delivery timelines. *For* *airline/travel* *services*,
> supply proof that the customer received the service (e.g. flight
> manifest showing the passenger traveled) 21 . In cases involving
> non-fungible tokens or cryptocurrency, Visa requires evidence of
> successful delivery to the wallet (e.g. blockchain transaction hash
> and destination wallet address) 22 .
>
> • **13.2** **–** **Cancelled** **Recurring** **Transaction:** The
> cardholder claims they were charged even after cancelling a recurring
> subscription or regular payment. **Required** **Evidence:** Proof that
> the cardholder did not properly cancel, or that the charge was
> authorized. For example, provide a copy of the cancellation policy
> that was disclosed to the cardholder and evidence that the cardholder
> did not follow it (if they failed to cancel in time) 23 24 . If the
> cardholder did cancel, show proof that you had not received any
> cancellation request before the charge, or that the charge occurred
> before the cancellation took effect. **Recommended** **Evidence:**
> Documentation of any communications or cancellation requests from the
> cardholder. If the cardholder never submitted a cancellation,
> providing usage logs of the service (showing the cardholder continued
> to use the service or did not opt out) can help. If the cardholder
> agreed to terms requiring a certain notice period to cancel, highlight
> the portion of the contract and show when (or if) the cardholder
> attempted to cancel relative to the billing date. In general,
> including a **signed** **or** **electronically** **agreed** service
> agreement with the recurring billing terms, and proof it was provided
> to the customer, is beneficial 25 23 .
>
> • **13.3** **–** **Not** **as** **Described** **or** **Defective**
> **Merchandise/Services:** The cardholder claims the product or service
> received was not as advertised, wrong, or was defective. **Required**
> **Evidence:** Proof that the goods or services matched the description
> provided at sale, or evidence refuting the defect claim. Provide any
> contracts, product descriptions, or terms that were agreed upon, and
> demonstrate that what was delivered conforms to those terms 26 . For
> example, supply photographs, specifications, or third-party
> inspections showing the item was authentic and as described if
> authenticity/quality is disputed 26 . If a service outcome is
> disputed, provide records of service delivery and the client’s
> acknowledgment of completion. **Recommended** **Evidence:** Customer
> correspondence (emails, chats) where the customer acknowledges
> satisfaction or never raised the issue prior to the chargeback. If the
> customer never attempted to return or report the problem, note that
> and provide your return/refund policy as it was disclosed 27 . Include
> any user manuals or usage logs if misuse could be a factor.
> Essentially, any **objective** **evidence** (photos, certificates of
> authenticity, repair reports) that the product was genuine and not
> defective will strengthen your case 26 . If the cardholder’s complaint
> is valid and you’ve already resolved it (e.g., provided a replacement
> or refund), show proof of that resolution.
>
> • **13.4** **–** **Counterfeit** **Merchandise:** The cardholder
> claims the goods received were counterfeit/ fake. **Required**
> **Evidence:** Proof that the merchandise is genuine/authentic as
> advertised. This can include manufacturer’s documentation or
> certifications of authenticity, serial numbers, or invoices from
> authorized distributors showing the goods are legitimate 28 .
> **Recommended** **Evidence:** Expert opinions or appraisals confirming
> authenticity, high-resolution photos of the item compared to genuine
> ones, and proof of the supply chain (e.g., your supplier invoices) to
> demonstrate the products came through authorized channels. If the
> customer never returned
>

>
> the item, you might note that as well (since counterfeit items are
> typically surrendered or destroyed in a claim). Provide your
> refund/return policy too, to show you offered a remedy.
>
> • **13.5** **–** **Misrepresentation:** The cardholder alleges that
> the merchant misrepresented the terms of sale or the product/service
> in a significant way (e.g., a bait-and-switch scenario). **Required**
> **Evidence:** Documentation proving the terms and conditions were
> clearly disclosed and that the merchant honored those terms. For
> example, if a customer claims a misrepresented refund policy or
> product feature, provide the original advertisement or contract and
> evidence that what was delivered met those terms 29 . **Recommended**
> **Evidence:** Copies of your website listing or brochure that the
> customer saw, screenshots of the checkout page showing the terms the
> cardholder agreed to (like *“no* *refunds”* or specific product
> details), and records of the cardholder acknowledging or accepting
> these terms (checkboxes, signatures) 30 23 . If the dispute revolves
> around a verbal claim, any written follow-up or email from you to the
> customer summarizing the terms can help. Also include any support
> correspondence where the customer’s understanding of the deal is
> evidenced, to show no wrongdoing on your part.
>
> • **13.6** **–** **Credit** **Not** **Processed:** The cardholder is
> claiming they were due a refund or credit (for a returned/cancelled
> item or agreed adjustment) which the merchant did not process.
> **Required** **Evidence:** Proof that a credit was already issued for
> the disputed amount, or that no credit is due. For instance, provide
> the credit transaction receipt or refund confirmation referencing the
> original charge 7 31 . If no credit was due, supply documents
> demonstrating why (e.g., the return did not meet your policy or the
> customer never actually returned the item). Include a written
> explanation if the cardholder is mistaken about expecting a credit 30
> 24 . **Recommended** **Evidence:** A timeline of events – e.g., order
> date, return date, refund date – with supporting paperwork (RMA forms,
> return tracking). If the refund was issued through a different channel
> (store credit, cash, etc.), provide evidence of that issuance or the
> customer’s acceptance of an alternative resolution. Also include the
> refund/cancellation policy that was provided to the customer at
> purchase, to show you followed the agreed terms.
>
> • **13.7** **–** **Cancelled** **Merchandise/Services:** The
> cardholder cancelled the transaction (not a recurring charge, but a
> one-time purchase or service) and claims they were still charged or
> not refunded. **Required** **Evidence:** Proof that the cardholder did
> not cancel within the allowed timeframe or that the goods/services
> were already delivered/used by the time of cancellation. For example,
> provide evidence of your cancellation policy disclosure (with any
> no-cancellation or cancellation-fee terms) and proof the cardholder
> didn’t meet those terms 23 32 . If the service was provided before
> cancellation, show that the customer benefited from the service (usage
> logs, access records). **Recommended** **Evidence:** Any communication
> from the cardholder regarding cancellation. If they never cancelled,
> state that and provide any relevant emails (or absence thereof). If
> they did cancel and you honored it by refunding, provide the refund
> proof. Also, receipts or check-in logs (for events, hotel stays, etc.)
> can show the customer *used* the service (hence a charge was valid
> even if they later asked for cancellation). In the case of tangible
> merchandise, if the item was already shipped before cancellation,
> provide the shipping proof and your policy about orders in transit.
>
> • **13.8** **–** **Original** **Credit** **Transaction** **Not**
> **Accepted:** This relates to Visa “Original Credit Transactions”
> (OCT) where the cardholder claims they never received a credit they
> were supposed to (often used for things like original credits or
> payouts). **Required** **Evidence:** Proof that the credit was
> properly processed and received by the cardholder’s account. Provide
> records of the OCT, including the date, amount, and the recipient
> account (PAN) showing it matches the cardholder’s card 33 34 . Often,
> a screenshot of the transaction detail from your processor or a letter
> from your acquirer confirming the credit posting sufices.
> **Recommended** **Evidence:**

> Communication with the cardholder (or recipient) acknowledging receipt
> of the funds (if available), or any bank statement snippet (if the
> cardholder provided it) showing the credit. If the credit failed and
> was re-issued via alternate means, evidence of that alternate
> resolution should be included.
>
> • **13.9** **–** **Non-Receipt** **of** **Cash** **at** **an**
> **ATM:** The cardholder attempted an ATM withdrawal or cash load using
> their card, but claims they did not receive the cash (or full amount)
> even though their account was charged. **Required** **Evidence:** ATM
> reconciliation records proving the machine dispensed the correct
> amount and there was no cash shortage. Typically, provide the ATM
> journal or electronic log for the transaction in question, showing
> cash dispensed successfully 35
>
> 36 . If the ATM had an error and later correction, provide evidence of
> any adjustments. **Recommended** **Evidence:** If you are the ATM
> owner/processor, include maintenance or balance check logs around that
> date to show no surplus cash was found. Security camera footage (if
> available) of the ATM during the transaction can be compelling if it
> clearly shows the cardholder taking the cash. If the cardholder
> eventually received the money via a correction, include proof of that
> credit.

> **Mastercard** **Chargeback** **Reason** **Codes**
>
> Mastercard updated its reason code system in recent years, using
> four-digit codes (often in the 4800s) for consumer disputes 40 . Below
> is a comprehensive list of Mastercard’s current chargeback reason
> codes (for transactions as of 2018 onward), with their meanings,
> required evidence per Mastercard guidelines, and recommended
> supporting evidence for a strong defense:
>
> • **4808** **–** **Authorization** **Related** **Chargeback** **(No**
> **Valid** **Authorization):** The transaction was not properly
> authorized (e.g., no approval or exceeded an approval amount).
> **Description:** The issuer is claiming the merchant either did not
> obtain an authorization or violated auth requirements (this replaces
> several older codes like 4807/4812) 41 . **Required** **Evidence:**
> Proof that a valid authorization approval was obtained for the full
> amount of the transaction 7 8 . This could be the authorization log or
> response code showing an approval for the exact transaction amount.
> Alternatively, proof of an already processed credit that offsets the
> charge can also refute the chargeback 42 . **Recommended**
> **Evidence:** Include the transaction receipt with the authorization
> code, any email/order confirmation showing the transaction date/time
> (to demonstrate it fell within any auth validity period). If multiple
> authorizations were involved (e.g., for split shipments or increased
> amounts), provide records of each and demonstrate compliance with
> Mastercard’s incremental auth rules. Essentially, any documentation
> showing you adhered to authorization rules will reinforce your case
> (such as showing an auth reversal for a canceled transaction, if
> relevant).
>

>
> • **4834** **–** **Point** **of** **Interaction** **Error**
> **(Processing** **Errors):** This is a broad code for errors at the
> point of interaction. It covers several scenarios like duplicate
> processing, incorrect transaction amount, transactions processed with
> the wrong currency or account, etc. **Description:** The cardholder or
> issuer asserts there was a processing mistake – for example, the
> cardholder was charged twice for the same item, charged an incorrect
> amount, was charged in the wrong currency, or an ATM error occurred 43
> 44 . **Required** **Evidence:** Because 4834 spans multiple
> sub-reasons, the required proof depends on the specific error:
>
> • *Duplicate* *Processing:* Show that the disputed charge is not a
> duplicate. Provide invoices or receipts demonstrating that two similar
> charges correspond to two distinct purchases 45 46 . If it was truly
> duplicate, show proof one of the transactions has already been
> refunded.
>
> • *Incorrect* *Transaction* *Amount:* Provide a copy of the
> transaction receipt or contract with the correct amount 45 . This
> proves the amount charged matched what the cardholder agreed to.
>
> • *Late* *Presentment:* Show the transaction was submitted within
> Mastercard’s time limits (e.g., provide the clearing date vs.
> transaction date) – evidence could be system printouts or acquirer
> confirmations.
>
> • *Currency* *Conversion* *Error:* Provide evidence the cardholder
> agreed to the currency (e.g., DCC consent proof) or that the posted
> currency is the same as on the receipt.
>
> • *ATM* *Dispute* *(incorrect* *dispensed* *amount):* ATM journals or
> reconciliation showing no surplus/ shortfall for that transaction.
>
> Mastercard’s rules often require *specific* *documents* for these
> scenarios. For instance, for a **duplicate** **charge** **(4834**
> **sub-code)**, the acquirer should prove two separate transactions
> occurred (different order/ reference numbers) 45 ; for **incorrect**
> **amount**, a signed receipt confirming the amount 47 ; for **paid**
> **by** **other** **means**, proof the cardholder’s other payment
> didn’t actually cover this transaction.
>
> **Recommended** **Evidence:** Provide any additional relevant docs: -
> Duplicate charge: detailed order records for each transaction,
> distinct timestamps, even cardholder communications acknowledging both
> purchases. - Incorrect amount: itemized bills, proof of any subsequent
> credit if an overcharge was identified and corrected. - Paid by other
> means: copies of your ledger or the customer’s check/cash receipt
> showing whether an alternate payment was received. If the customer’s
> claim is incorrect, a statement like *“no* *record* *of* *cash*
> *payment* *was* *found”* along with internal audit logs supports your
> case. -Late presentment: correspondence with your processor about
> submission delays (if any external factor caused late submission).
>
> Essentially, align your evidence with the **exact** **error**
> **claimed**. Mastercard’s chargeback guide notes that supporting
> documentation (like transaction receipts, proof of cardholder
> agreement, etc.) *should* *be* *provided* to demonstrate the
> transaction was processed correctly 43 46 .
>
> • **4837** **–** **No** **Cardholder** **Authorization**
> **(Fraudulent** **Transaction):** The cardholder denies participating
> in or authorizing the transaction (classic fraud claim).
> **Description:** This code is used when the cardholder says, *“I*
> *didn’t* *authorize* *this* *charge.”* It includes lost/stolen card
> use in a non-chip scenario or other unauthorized transactions in
> card-present or card-not-present environments 48 . **Required**
> **Evidence:** Proof that the transaction was properly authorized by
> the cardholder. For card-present, this may mean evidence of an imprint
> or swipe and a match on the signature, or that PIN was used (if PIN
> debit/Maestro). For e-commerce, evidence could be an AVS match or CVV
> match, or ideally 3-D Secure authentication indicating the
> cardholder’s identity was verified. If the charge was *3-D* *Secure*
> *authenticated* *or* *had* *an* *approved* *identity* *verification*,
> highlight that (Mastercard considers that compelling for fraud
> disputes). Also, if you have any indication the cardholder themselves
> performed the transaction (device ID, IP address matching
>
> 7
>
> their prior orders, etc.), include it. **Recommended** **Evidence:**
> Any **compelling** **evidence** as defined by Mastercard. This can
> include:
>
> • Proof of previous successful, undisputed transactions with the same
> card and merchant (especially if those goods/services were delivered
> to the same address or used by the cardholder) – demonstrating a
> history can rebut “first-time” fraud claims 49 .
>
> • Delivery confirmation with the cardholder’s name and address for
> shipped goods.
>
> • For digital goods, logs showing the cardholder’s account (username,
> email) accessed the product or service.
>
> • Communication from the cardholder (emails, support tickets) related
> to the transaction (e.g., if they contacted you for support
> post-purchase, it indicates participation).
>
> In short, provide evidence that ties the cardholder to the
> transaction. For instance, “IP address X was used for this purchase
> and matches the IP from the cardholder’s prior login” or “The
> customer’s account on our platform (email: example@domain.com) placed
> this order and had a password-protected login.” Mastercard’s
> guidelines allow a representment if you have two or more pieces of
> compelling evidence showing the cardholder’s identity or acceptance 50

> • **4849** **–** **Questionable** **Merchant** **Activity:** This code
> is triggered typically by the issuer or network when the merchant’s
> activities are deemed suspicious (e.g., a high number of fraud claims
> or compliance issues – it’s a bit of a catch-all similar to Visa’s
> “Misrepresentation” or “Compliance” categories). **Description:**
> Discovering patterns like excessive fraud or misclassification, the
> network/issuer disputes transactions en masse. (Note: 4849 is often
> seen when Mastercard intervenes due to merchant fraud or policy
> violations). **Required** **Evidence:** It can be challenging – the
> merchant needs to prove that the transactions in question are
> legitimate and that they have not violated any rules. This might
> involve providing proof of cardholder authorization for each disputed
> transaction and proof of delivery/fulfillment. **Recommended**
> **Evidence:** Because this reason is broad, respond with
> *comprehensive* *documentation*: copies of all transaction records,
> customer correspondence, proof of delivery, your business licenses or
> evidence you followed required procedures. Essentially, you want to
> demonstrate your operations are legitimate. If this code arises from a
> **cardholder** **dispute** **pattern**, treat each transaction like a
> fraud dispute and provide the relevant compelling evidence as above.
> If it arises from a **compliance** **audit** **issue**, provide
> documentation that you corrected any violations and that the
> cardholder did receive what they paid for.

> • **4853** **–** **Cardholder** **Dispute** **(Generic):** Mastercard
> consolidated many of its consumer dispute codes into 4853. This
> umbrella covers a range of cardholder dissatisfaction claims: goods/

> not processed, canceled recurring, etc. The network expects additional
> “dispute information” from the issuer to clarify the nature
> (Mastercard issuers attach condition codes or descriptions).
> **Description:** A general code indicating the cardholder has a
> dispute unrelated to fraud or auth – basically a service or product

> • **Goods/Services** **Not** **Provided** **(Non-receipt):**
> Cardholder claims they paid but got nothing. **Required**
> **Evidence:** Proof of delivery or service fulfillment, similar to
> Visa 13.1. Provide delivery tracking showing the item delivered to the
> cardholder’s address with date/signature 46 17 . For services, provide
> logs or evidence the service was available/used (e.g., access logins,
> appointment records). If the service/event is in the future and not
> yet occurred, evidence that it’s still scheduled (to show the claim is
> premature) can also counter the chargeback. **Recommended**
> **Evidence:** Customer communications acknowledging receipt or
> scheduling, photos of delivered goods at the recipient’s location, or
> a signed work order for services
>

> what they purchased (or that they prematurely filed a chargeback) will

> • **Not** **as** **Described** **/** **Defective** **Merchandise:**
> Cardholder says what they got is not what was promised or is faulty.
> **Required** **Evidence:** Proof that the product or service was
> delivered as described and in working order. This can include
> contracts, item descriptions, and evidence of conformity. For example,
> if the customer claims a different color or version, show the order
> detail they agreed to and that you delivered that exact item. If they
> claim defect, provide any quality checks or photos showing the item
> before shipping, or proof the item works (if applicable).

> cardholder did not mention issues, or troubleshooting emails where it
> appears user error might be at fault. Provide your return policy; if
> the customer never attempted to return or contact you for a fix, note
> that and provide that policy showing they had avenues for resolution.
>
> • **Cancelled** **Merchandise/Service** **(single** **transaction,**
> **not** **recurring):** Similar to Visa 13.7. **Required**
> **Evidence:** Proof the cardholder’s cancellation request was either
> not received or not eligible (e.g., came too late), or that you did
> process a refund per policy. Provide the timestamp of any cancellation
> and compare with your policy. **Recommended** **Evidence:** Logs from
> your system showing no cancellation request was made before
> shipment/service. If a cancellation was made and you issued a credit,
> include proof of that credit to show the dispute is moot. Also include
> the policy that the cardholder agreed to (with their acknowledgment if
> possible).
>
> • **Credit** **Not** **Processed:** Cardholder returned an item or was
> promised a refund that they haven’t gotten. **Required** **Evidence:**
> Proof that you did issue the credit (include credit transaction
> receipts or refund confirmation) 7 , or proof that the cardholder is
> not entitled to one (e.g., return never happened, or was outside
> policy). **Recommended** **Evidence:** Internal refund ledger showing
> the credit, any email to the customer confirming the refund, or, if no
> refund was due, evidence of why (e.g., item not returned as claimed,
> which you can back up with photos of the product if the customer kept
> it). If the refund is in process or delayed for some reason, provide
> documentation of that communication.
>
> • **Recurring** **Transaction** **Dispute:** Cardholder claims they
> canceled a subscription (or similar recurring payment) and were still
> charged (similar to Visa 13.2). **Required** **Evidence:** Proof that
> either the cardholder didn’t cancel per terms or that you have already
> honored a cancellation. Provide the recurring agreement terms (with
> cardholder’s sign-up confirmation), and evidence of usage if they
> continued to use the service. **Recommended** **Evidence:** System
> records of when the cardholder canceled (or that they never did). If
> they canceled after the billing cycle started, show that timing. If
> you have an email from the customer about canceling after the charge
> date, include that to show the charge was per terms. Any notification
> emails you sent about upcoming renewals can also demonstrate you
> informed the customer in advance.

> • **4855** **–** **Transaction** **Did** **Not** **Complete:** Often

> **Description:** A counterfeit card was used at a non-EMV terminal,

> was actually EMV chip-read (provide terminal logs proving chip read)
> or that it falls under an exemption. If your terminal was chip-enabled
> and it was a fallback transaction, show documentation of the chip read
> attempt and fallback reason. **Recommended** **Evidence:** If you
> suspect the claim is false (perhaps the cardholder actually did the
> purchase), you can provide any fraud-related compelling evidence
> (security video, etc.), but generally 4870 chargebacks are not
> reversible unless a mistake was made (e.g., the card was non-chip or
> an EMV credit was obtained). If you do have a signed receipt or other
> evidence, include it, but know that under the rules the issuer is
> empowered to chargeback counterfeit fraud in non-EMV scenarios. The
> best “evidence” here is proof of EMV compliance: if you can show the
> transaction was chip-read or was an contactless CDCVM transaction
> (which counts as chip), it shouldn’t have been charged back under 4870

> **(Lost/Stolen/NRI):** Similar to 4870, but for lost/stolen or

> (or perhaps where PIN should have been used). **Required**
> **Evidence:** Also dificult to contest – the primary defense is
> showing EMV was used or that PIN was entered (for lost/stolen, an
> online PIN used shifts liability back to issuer). If you have evidence
> the cardholder participated (e.g., a PIN entered, which for lost card
> would implicate the cardholder), provide that. **Recommended**
> **Evidence:** Same approach as 4870: any surveillance, signed
> receipts, etc. to show the legitimate cardholder’s involvement might
> sway an issuer before arbitration, but by rules, these are hard to
> win. Focus on demonstrating compliance (if any) or providing context
> if the chargeback was misfiled.

> **American** **Express** **(Amex)** **Chargeback** **Reason**
> **Codes**
>
> American Express uses an alphanumeric reason code system where a
> leading letter denotes the category (A = Authorization, C = Cardmember
> Disputes, F = Fraud, P = Processing Errors, R = Misc/ Other). Below
> are all **current** **Amex** **consumer** **chargeback** **reason**
> **codes** with their descriptions, required evidence per Amex’s
> oficial Merchant Regulations, and recommended supporting documents:
>
> • **A01** **–** **Charge** **Amount** **Exceeds** **Authorization**
> **Amount:** The transaction amount was higher than what was approved
> in the authorization. **Required** **Evidence:** Proof that you
> obtained an authorization for the full charged amount 7 . For example,
> provide the authorization approval code details showing the amount
> approved equals the transaction amount, or documentation that multiple
> incremental authorizations were obtained to cover the total.
> Alternatively, if an authorization was not obtained for the full
> amount, evidence that an *exception* *applies* (such as a valid reason
> the amount increased per Amex rules) or that the charge was split
> properly. **Recommended** **Evidence:** Include the transaction
> receipt/invoice showing the amount the Cardmember signed for, along
> with the authorization log. If you already processed a partial credit
> to bring the amount within the authorized limit, include proof of
> that. Any correspondence with Amex (or the processor) acknowledging a
> correction or exception can also support your case. Essentially,
> demonstrate either compliance or remediation – e.g., *“Customer*
> *agreed* *to* *additional* *\$X* *charge* *on* *top* *of* *authorized*
> *amount,* *and* *a* *separate* *auth* *was* *obtained”* (provide that
> separate auth or customer agreement).
>
> • **A02** **–** **No** **Valid** **Authorization:** Amex has no record
> that the charge was authorized (or it was outright declined and still
> charged). **Required** **Evidence:** Proof that a valid authorization
> approval was obtained before charging the card 54 55 . This could be
> an authorization approval code or screenshot from your terminal logs
> showing “Approved” with the date/time for that
>
> 11
>
> Cardmember’s account. If the charge was a result of a processing error
> (e.g., a batch was sent without auth), you may not have evidence – in
> such cases, this chargeback is typically valid unless you can show the
> issuer’s system had an outage (rare). **Recommended** **Evidence:** If
> you have any email or communication from Amex or your acquirer about
> system issues that prevented auth, include that. Otherwise, your best
> bet is proof of an auth code. If an auth wasn’t obtained due to a
> misunderstanding (e.g., you thought a previous auth was still valid),
> you can show any related documentation, but Amex is strict on this. If
> the Cardmember received the goods, sometimes showing they got the
> merchandise doesn’t overturn A02, but it may support a goodwill case –
> include proof of delivery to at least demonstrate it wasn’t
> fraudulent, if you’re pursuing goodwill or arbitration.

> • **A08** **–** **Authorization** **Approval** **Expired:** The
> authorization was valid but had expired by the time the transaction
> was submitted (authorizations on Amex have time limits). **Required**
> **Evidence:** Proof that the transaction was submitted within Amex’s
> allowable timeframe, or that a new authorization was obtained. Provide
> the timestamp of the authorization and the timestamp of when you
> submitted the charge, to show it was within the window (if it was) 56
> 54 . If beyond the window, evidence that you obtained a *fresh*
> *approval* before charging (or that the charge date on your records is
> different due to a delay in Amex processing). **Recommended**
> **Evidence:** Transaction logs from your POS or gateway showing the
> sequence (auth date and settlement date). If a delay occurred due to
> an Amex outage or per Cardmember request (e.g., they wanted you to
> hold and combine charges), any written proof of that can help as
> mitigating evidence. However, typically if you missed the window, the
> chargeback will stand unless Amex grants an exception.
>
> • **C02** **–** **Credit** **Not** **Processed:** The Cardmember is
> claiming they never received a refund/credit they were promised or due
> (similar to Visa 13.6). **Required** **Evidence:** Proof that you
> *did* *issue* the credit for the disputed transaction, or evidence
> that the Cardmember isn’t entitled to a credit. For example, provide a
> copy of the credit receipt or screenshot from your system showing the
> credit (with date and amount) back to the Cardmember’s Amex card 57 58
> . If no credit was issued because the Cardmember never returned the
> item (or returned it outside the policy), provide documentation of
> your refund policy and evidence the Cardmember did not meet the
> conditions (e.g. no record of return in your system, or return was
> past the deadline). **Recommended** **Evidence:** Any communication
> with the Cardmember about the refund – for instance, an email
> confirming “Your refund has been processed on X date” along with the
> transaction reference. If the refund was issued via a different method
> (store credit, cash, etc.), provide proof of that issuance and the
> Cardmember’s acceptance. Also include the original sale and return
> receipts to create a clear timeline (purchase on Date A, return on
> Date B, credit issued on Date C). This clarity can help Amex see if
> maybe the credit was in process or the Cardmember was mistaken.
>
> • **C04** **–** **Goods/Services** **Returned** **or** **Refused:**
> The Cardmember returned merchandise or refused services, but didn’t
> get a refund (or a cancellation credit). **Required** **Evidence:**
> Proof that you have not received the returned goods or that a refund
> was already processed. If you did get the return, show that you’ve
> issued the credit (or why the return was not eligible). If you never
> received a return shipment, provide evidence of that – for example, a
> statement: “Item was never returned to us,” ideally supported by lack
> of any RMA or warehouse intake records. If your policy was “final
> sale” and the customer agreed, provide that policy (as disclosed) and
> evidence the customer knew it (e.g., marked on the receipt or checkbox
> online) 30 23 . **Recommended** **Evidence:** Shipping records or
> tracking numbers. For instance, if the customer claims they mailed
> back an item, you can show you have no record of it – or if you do
> have a record, show when it was received and whether a credit was
> issued. If the dispute is about refused delivery

> non-receipt: provide proof of delivery or service provision (tracking

> **partially** **received**, show that you delivered the portion that
> was shipped and perhaps clarify remaining items’ status (backorder

> disputes, include any emails where the Cardmember requested

> phone cancellation, a written summary helps. Show the terms of sale
> regarding cancellation (e.g., *“must* *cancel* *24* *hours* *before”*
> or *“tickets* *are* *non-refundable”*). For non-receipt, include the
> same kind of supporting docs as Visa cases: proof of delivery (with
> signature if available), and correspondence (like the Cardmember’s

> paid for” dispute. **Required** **Evidence:** Proof of delivery or
> completion of service as agreed. Provide shipping confirmation,
> tracking number, delivery signature, or evidence of digital content
> delivery (e.g., download/ access logs) 60 61 . If partially delivered,

> be refunded/voided. **Recommended** **Evidence:** Photographic proof

> Cardmember of receipt (for instance, a support ticket: “I got the item
> but I’m unhappy with it” – which proves they *did* get it). Also
> helpful is proof of fulfillment from third-party systems (for example,
> if it was sent via a courier service, a copy of the delivery
> confirmation from their system). If the Cardmember picked up goods
> in-store, a copy of the pickup slip or ID verification record. For

> claims they paid using another method (cash, check, another card) and

> **Evidence:** Proof that the Amex charge was valid and not a duplicate
> of another payment. For example, show that the Amex payment was the
> only payment received for that transaction – no record of cash or
> other card for the same invoice 62 63 . If the Cardmember indeed paid
> twice (once by Amex, once by alternate method), you should have

> check.” If you have access to evidence like “the check they wrote
> bounced” or “we never received a check,” include that info. If they
> claim to have paid by another card, you obviously wouldn’t have that
> other card’s info, but you can provide the invoice and note it remains

> **Cancelled:** The Cardholder was charged for a guaranteed reservation

> cardholder either did not cancel according to policy or that they
> agreed to the no-show terms. For hotels (no-show), provide a copy of
> the reservation terms the Cardmember agreed to, which typically state
> the cancellation deadline and no-show charge amount. Also provide

> showing the status was still confirmed and unused). For a car rental
> deposit (CAR deposit) that the Cardmember says was canceled, similar
> evidence: the cancellation policy and proof the Cardmember didn’t meet
> it, or that you never received a cancellation. **Recommended**
> **Evidence:** A copy of the email or fax confirmation sent to the
> Cardmember with the reservation details and cancellation policy, or
> the webpage snippet where the Cardmember booked (with timestamp) that
> shows *“Cancel* *by* *6pm* *on* *X* *or* *one* *night* *will* *be*
> *charged.”* If the Cardmember had a cancellation number, Amex might
> have provided it to you in the dispute – if you see one and you have a
> record that the cancellation was late/invalid, address it explicitly

> deadline, as shown by the timestamp”). If the Cardmember did *use* the
> service (e.g., they actually picked up the rental car but are claiming
> otherwise), provide the rental agreement or hotel check-in folio they
> signed. Essentially, prove either that the **no-show** **charge**
> **was** **policy-compliant** or that the customer actually took the
> service (making the charge valid).

> • **C28** **–** **Canceled** **Recurring** **Billing:** The Cardmember
> states they canceled a recurring subscription or automatic billing but
> were still charged (similar to Visa 13.2, and akin to C05 in domestic
> US code). **Required** **Evidence:** Proof that either the Cardmember
> didn’t follow the cancellation procedure or that you were never
> notified of cancellation before the charge. Supply the enrollment
> terms for the recurring billing where the cancellation process is
> described, and evidence the Cardmember agreed (e.g., checked a box
> online or signed an agreement) 23 32 . Then provide your records of
> that account: no cancellation request logged before the disputed
> charge date. If you did receive a cancellation and mistakenly billed,
> you’d normally just refund – fighting it would require showing the
> charge happened *before* the cancellation was effective.

> the Cardmember’s account (active vs. canceled) and the date/time of
> any cancellation request. If the Cardmember canceled via phone or
> email, any correspondence around that is key. For example, if they
> emailed cancel on Jan 5 but their billing date was Jan 1, and you
> charged on Jan 1, include that context and your policy (which likely
> says “to avoid next charge, cancel 5 days prior,” etc.). If you have
> customer service notes (e.g., “Jan 10: customer called to cancel, we
> informed them charge on Jan 1 is non-refundable”), that can be
> compelling to show you handled it per policy. Additionally, evidence
> that the Cardmember used the service after the disputed charge (like
> logged in, or kept the product) can indicate the charge was rightful
> until cancellation.

> • **C31** **–** **Goods/Services** **Not** **as** **Described:** The
> Cardmember claims what they received is different from what was
> advertised or expected (e.g., wrong item, materially different,
> misleading description). **Required** **Evidence:** Proof that the
> product/service matched the description the Cardmember saw at the time
> of purchase. Provide the original item description, contract, or
> advertisement, and then show how what you delivered corresponds
> exactly to that. If the dispute is about quality or functionality not
> matching claims, include documentation such as product specifications,
> photographs of the delivered item, or service deliverables that align
> with what was promised. **Recommended** **Evidence:** If possible, a
> statement or acknowledgment from the Cardmember (maybe in an email or
> review) that initially they were aware of what they were getting. For
> example, if they claim color or size was wrong, provide evidence of
> the options they selected. If they claim a feature was missing, show
> the specs sheet highlighting that feature and any evidence that the
> product indeed has it. Also, your **return** **policy** again – if the
> customer never sought a return or exchange, note that (Amex will
> wonder if it truly was wrong, why didn’t they return it?). Including
> any customer communication where they complain for the first time via
> chargeback (and never gave you a chance to resolve) can support that
> you weren’t aware of any issue. Essentially, prove the item/service
> was as described, and that the Cardmember’s expectations (as set by
> your description) should have been met.
>
> • **C32** **–** **Goods/Services** **Damaged** **or** **Defective:**
> The Cardmember received the product but claims it was damaged,
> defective, or not working upon arrival. **Required** **Evidence:**
> Proof that the merchandise was functional and not defective when
> delivered, or that the damage was not due to the merchant. This can be
> challenging because it’s often one person’s word against another’s. If
> you have a QA process, provide records (e.g., testing certifications,
> photos of the item in good condition before shipping). If it’s a
> service that’s claimed defective (e.g., repair didn’t fix issue),
> provide evidence of completion and perhaps any sign-off by the
> Cardmember. **Recommended** **Evidence:** Photographs are great here –
> if you have packaging photos or if the shipping carrier has data that
> the package was delivered intact (or the weight matching an undamaged
> item), include that. Also, any warranty or repair offers you extended
> to the customer – if the customer didn’t take advantage, it shows
> possibly they jumped to chargeback. If the Cardmember never reported
> the problem to you, mention that and provide your policy that
> instructs them to contact you for damage within X days. If they did
> contact you and you offered a remedy which they refused, provide those
> communications. Additionally, if you have other customers and none
> reported a similar defect (for a product), you could indirectly hint
> that the item wasn’t generally faulty (though such statements carry
> less weight than direct evidence). In summary, demonstrate due
> diligence on your side and possibly customer’s lack of cooperation in
> resolving the issue.
>
> • **F29** **–** **Card** **Not** **Present** **Fraud:** The Cardmember
> claims fraud on an online or mail/phone transaction (Amex F29
> indicates an unauthorized CNP transaction) 64 65 . **Required**
> **Evidence:** This is similar to other networks’ fraud disputes.
> Provide proof of Cardmember authentication or participation: AVS
> match, CVV match, IP address, any 3D Secure (SafeKey) authentication
> results, etc. For instance, evidence that the billing/shipping address
> matched the Cardmember’s on file, or that the email used is the
> Cardmember’s, can be part of compelling evidence. **Recommended**
> **Evidence:** Amex sometimes allows what they call “Fraud Full
> Recourse” on certain transactions, meaning you may not win simply by
> evidence if the account was truly compromised. However, if you have
> compelling evidence (e.g., the Cardmember previously successfully
> transacted with you online from the same device/IP, or they logged
> into an existing account with you to make the purchase), present that.
> Provide delivery proof as well – if merchandise was delivered to the
> Cardmember’s verified address or was picked up by them (with ID
> verification perhaps), include that. Essentially, build a case that
> the transaction was legitimate: “Card was not present, but here are
> the safeguards we passed: CVV matched 66 , AVS matched, and item
> delivered to card’s
>
> 15
>
> billing address with signature.” Also mention if you have an agreement
> to participate in Amex’s fraud programs (like if it was an Amex
> SafeKey authenticated transaction – though Amex’s program isn’t as
> widely used as Visa/MC). The more ties to the Cardmember, the better.

> • **F30** **–** **EMV** **Counterfeit** **Fraud** **Liability**
> **Shift:** Similar to Mastercard 4870 – a counterfeit card used in
> person, liability falls on the non-chip-capable party. **Required**
> **Evidence:** If you had an EMV device and the chip was used or the
> card was non-chip, you might fight this by showing your compliance.
> But typically, Amex will not side with the merchant if you didn’t
> process via chip. So evidence might include proof the transaction was
> chip-read (if indeed your system indicates it was). **Recommended**
> **Evidence:** Much like MC/Visa, there’s little to do if you weren’t
> chip-compliant. If you were, then it could be a mistake – show your
> terminal’s EMV log. If the chip failed and you have a swipe, show that
> and any error codes to possibly pursue an error in card coding.
> Otherwise, additional evidence (signed receipt, etc.) likely won’t
> overturn the liability shift if it squarely applies.
>
> • **F31** **–** **EMV** **Lost/Stolen/NRI** **Fraud** **Liability**
> **Shift:** Similar idea but for lost/stolen where PIN should have been
> used. **Required** **Evidence:** Proof that the transaction was
> conducted with chip + PIN (if it was, then liability may shift back to
> issuer). If your records show PIN was entered and verified, supply
> that. **Recommended** **Evidence:** Same caution as F30 – if you
> didn’t follow EMV/ PIN, not much can overturn it. If you did, show it
> (e.g., an authorization response that says “AUTH CODE: XYZ, Chip/PIN
> verified”).

> • **R03** **–** **Insuficient** **Reply** **to** **Inquiry:** Amex
> first often sends an “Inquiry” (also called a Request for Information
> or Chargeback Inquiry) asking the merchant for details before
> escalating to chargeback. R03 means the merchant’s response to an
> inquiry was deemed insuficient or was not received in time 67 68 .
> **Required** **Evidence:** If this becomes a chargeback, it means Amex
> thought you didn’t fulfill the info request. To fight R03, you’d need
> to prove you actually responded adequately and on time. Provide copies
> of what you sent to Amex (e.g., a fax confirmation or email with the
> info) and any Amex acknowledgment. **Recommended** **Evidence:** If
> you have a case number or reference from the inquiry, use that in your
> correspondence now. Essentially, you’re appealing to Amex that you
> *did* comply. Include the full package of documents that were
> requested (sales receipt, proof of delivery, etc. depending on the
> original inquiry reason) – at this stage, even if you sent it before,
> send it again, making it as clear and complete as possible. A cover
> letter explaining that you did respond timely with the attached
> information can help the reviewer reconsider. If you missed the
> deadline but have a very good reason (system outage, etc.), mention
> that and include proof if possible. However, R03 is often not
> overturned unless there’s evidence Amex missed your response.
>
> • **R13** **–** **No** **Reply** **to** **Inquiry:** This indicates
> you did not respond *at* *all* to an Amex inquiry within the required
> timeframe 69 70 . **Required** **Evidence:** Similar to R03, you’d
> have to show that you actually did send a response. Provide
> dates/times and copies of your response. If you truly missed it,
> unfortunately this chargeback is typically not reversible – Amex rules
> treat non-response as acceptance of the chargeback. **Recommended**
> **Evidence:** If you have any proof that extenuating circumstances
> prevented your reply (and perhaps you tried to resolve directly with
> the Cardmember), you could include that and plead for reversal, but
> success is unlikely. Focus on internal process improvement to avoid
> R13 in future; for the record, if you *did* send a response and have
> clear proof Amex received it, then present that vigorously.

> • **P01** **–** **Unassigned** **Card** **Number:** The card number
> used was not actually issued (invalid number), yet the transaction was
> processed – perhaps due to a key-entry error or fraud with fabricated
> number. **Required** **Evidence:** There’s almost no representment
> path for an unassigned number; it basically means the merchant didn’t
> get a valid auth. If it’s a data entry mistake (you typed wrong number
> that coincidentally passed mod 10 and got auth approval), you could
> show that you actually did receive an auth approval (though how, if
> unassigned?). Typically, this is a straightforward chargeback.
> **Recommended** **Evidence:** Not applicable, except if you suspect
> Amex’s assertion is wrong (maybe the number was valid and they made an
> error). You could ask your processor to double-check the PAN. Usually,
> you accept this chargeback or pursue your customer outside of Amex.
>
> • **P03** **–** **Credit** **Processed** **as** **Charge:** The
> Cardmember was expecting a refund but a charge went through instead
> (for example, you accidentally ran a refund as a positive sale).
> **Required** **Evidence:** Proof that this was a processing error that
> has been corrected, or that the Cardmember is misunderstanding. The
> best evidence is a documentation that you have since processed the
> proper credit. For instance, *if* *you* *already* *caught* *the*
> *mistake* *and* *refunded*, provide the credit receipt. If you haven’t
> yet, you might just issue the credit rather than fight. If the
> Cardmember is wrong (not common in this scenario), show the
> transaction records (perhaps they saw a charge and thought it was
> supposed to be a refund for a different transaction). **Recommended**
> **Evidence:** A letter explaining the situation can accompany any
> proof. E.g., “Transaction on 01/05 was mistakenly run as a sale for
> \$100 instead of a credit. We have now processed a \$100 credit on
> 01/10 (see attached).” With that, Amex will likely rebill the
> Cardmember if the credit has already offset it. If it was a
> misunderstanding (like two offsetting entries), show both the charge
> and credit and that one netted the other.
>
> • **P04** **–** **Charge** **Processed** **as** **Credit:** The
> opposite error – you intended to charge but it went through as a
> credit, yet somehow the Cardmember’s account shows a debit? (This one
> is rare; more often it’s an internal issue, not resulting in
> chargeback since Cardmember wouldn’t complain about a credit.) If it
> does come up, likely the issuer is adjusting something. **Required**
> **Evidence:** If you truly did the transaction incorrectly, you might
> need to recharge properly (with auth) – but as a chargeback, maybe
> issuer is reclaiming a wrong credit. In any case, evidence would be
> internal logs showing what happened. **Recommended** **Evidence:** N/A
> beyond internal processing records; treat on case-by-case basis with
> your acquirer.
>
> • **P05** **–** **Incorrect** **Charge** **Amount:** Similar to
> C31/C32 in concept but specifically that the numeric amount is wrong
> due to a processing error (not an authorization issue but maybe a key
> entry error). **Required** **Evidence:** Proof of the correct agreed
> amount (customer’s receipt/order) and that you charged that amount. If
> you accidentally overcharged, it’s best to just credit the difference
> rather than fight. But if you didn’t, show the signed receipt for the
> correct amount 71 . **Recommended** **Evidence:** If the dispute is
> small difference (like tip not added correctly or such), provide the
> receipt showing what they wrote. If it’s a misunderstanding (customer
> thought price was lower), provide the invoice/contract. If you already
> refunded the difference, show that.
>
> • **P07** **–** **Late** **Submission:** The transaction was submitted
> to Amex beyond the allowed timeframe (generally 7 days for most
> charges in Amex, though this can vary). **Required** **Evidence:**
> Proof of the transaction date and the submission/processing date to
> show it was within the limit (if it indeed was). For example, if the
> charge date on the receipt is Jan 1 and you submitted Jan 5, that’s
> timely – provide those records. If it was actually late (say submitted
> Feb 10), it’s hard to fight since rules are clear. **Recommended**
> **Evidence:** If you have any Amex communication granting an exception
> or if the Cardmember received the goods and benefited (though that
> doesn’t change the technical violation, sometimes Amex might show
> leniency if it’s not
>
> 17
>
> egregious). But typically, if late, accept the chargeback. If fighting
> due to misidentification (Amex thought it was late but it wasn’t),
> provide timestamps from your batch and any processor acknowledgment
> showing the date submitted.
>
> • **P08** **–** **Duplicate** **Charge:** The Cardmember got charged
> twice for the same transaction. **Required** **Evidence:** Proof that
> there were in fact two distinct transactions or that one of the
> charges has already been refunded. Provide receipts/invoices for each
> charge demonstrating they cover different goods/services or different
> dates 45 46 . If it truly was a duplicate in error and you refunded
> one, show the refund documentation. **Recommended** **Evidence:** A
> clear explanation letter if it was not actually a duplicate: e.g.,
> “The customer made two separate purchases: one on Jan 1 (Ref \#123)
> and another on Jan 2 (Ref \#124). Attached are both signed receipts.”
> Highlight differences (maybe one is a separate family member’s
> purchase). If it was a duplicate, don’t contest; just ensure the
> Cardmember is credited (either by chargeback or your own refund). If
> you refunded pre-emptively, show that to possibly stop the chargeback.

> history: when did they sign up, how were they told to cancel, and do
> you have any record of a cancellation? If none, state that. If they
> did cancel, show when and how, and if the disputed charge fell before

> the Cardmember canceled on a website, show the confirmation page or

> *cancellation* *call/email* *from* *the* *Cardmember* *prior* *to*

> clearly states the billing cycle and cancellation requirements. If you
> have any usage logs indicating the Cardmember continued to use the

> were aware service was active”). If you already issued a refund as a

> to a distinct purchase or service. Provide receipts or work orders for
> each charge, highlighting differences like different dates, order
> numbers, or items 86 87 . If it truly was duplicate, your only defense
> is if you already refunded one of them – in which case show that
> refund. **Recommended** **Evidence:** A written explanation if the

> was your error (you batched the same payment twice), best to concede

> • **RG** **–** **Non-Receipt** **of** **Goods** **or** **Services:**

> tracking information showing the item was delivered to the
> Cardmember’s address (with date and preferably a signature or delivery
> confirmation) 17 18 . For services, provide logs or evidence the
> service was made available/used (e.g., for an airline ticket, proof
> they checked in or flew; for an e-ticket, proof it was emailed and not
> bounced). If an item was picked up, provide a pickup receipt or signed
> collection form. **Recommended** **Evidence:** Discover specifically
> advises providing as much evidence as possible that the Cardmember
> indeed got the goods 90 89 . In addition to tracking, include any
> emails from the Cardmember after delivery (perhaps thanking or asking
> a follow-up question – indicating receipt). If you require login to
> access a service (like an online course), provide logs showing the

> premature (filed before expected delivery date), provide the order
> showing the expected ship date and note it wasn’t due yet (and now

> **Described/Defective):** The Cardmember received the product but

> goods/services were delivered as described and in good condition.
> Provide the product description or advertisement and show that what

> that you did obtain a proper authorization for the transaction.
> Provide the authorization code and details (date/time, amount) for the
> Discover transaction in question. If it was a case of not obtaining
> auth, unless you have a network error evidence, this will be hard to
> overturn. **Recommended** **Evidence:** If you did get an auth but
> perhaps on a second attempt, provide whatever evidence of
> communication with Discover’s auth system. If the issue was an
> incorrect response to a referral or something, you can argue by
> showing you ultimately got an approval. But largely, for
> missing/invalid auth, it’s best to show any mitigating evidence (like
> the charge was small and under your floor limit – though with Discover
> typically all should be authorized). If you inadvertently skipped auth
> but the card was good, maybe provide a record that the card was in
> good standing (if you somehow know). However, in most cases, you’d
> accept this chargeback or hope for issuer leniency if the customer
> actually got the goods. Not much compelling evidence can override a
> clear auth violation.

> a strong case that Discover’s communication failed (e.g., wrong

> **Conclusion:** In all cases across Visa, Mastercard, Amex, and
> Discover, the key to winning a chargeback dispute is to **meet**
> **the** **network’s** **required** **evidence** **criteria** and then
> **exceed** **it** **with** **additional** **compelling** **evidence**.
> Organize your response by clearly citing the reason code and attaching
> relevant documentation. Use tables or bullet points in your cover
> letter to map each piece of evidence to a specific claim by the
> cardholder or requirement by the network. By following the oficial
> guidelines for required documents – such as proof of authorization,
> proof of delivery, contracts, receipts, etc. – and including
> supporting evidence like communications and logs, you maximize your
> chances of a successful representment 5 53 . Always double-check that
> you are using the latest dispute reason code reference for each
> network, as codes and rules can update over time.
